Research & Method

Mnemosyne Viva

An open research atlas where images, historical sources, concepts, and arguments are explored together

The Long-Term Atlas

Mnemosyne Viva (hosted at iconocracia.com) is a long-term research project conceived to make legal-historical and visual research accessible without flattening its complexity. Rather than acting as a closed container for a single dissertation, the atlas brings together a transnational repertoire of female allegories with explicit politico-juridical functions, organized so that new strands, periods, and comparative essays can continue developing over time.

Across every surface, the project preserves a strict epistemic boundary: it distinguishes documented objects and historical sources (archival provenance, cataloged dates, material support, institutional custody, and reproduction credits) from authorial interpretations (conceptual readings, iconocratic regimes, thematic constellations, and interactive experiments).

The image of Justice is a woman. The image of the Republic is a woman. The image of the Nation is a woman. And yet it is upon the bodies of living women that the legal order erected itself by excluding them.

The Doctoral Strand: Mármore à Margem (Brazil, 1822–1922)

Within the broader comparative atlas, the doctoral dissertation developed by Ana Vanzin ↗ at the Graduate Program in Law of the Federal University of Santa Catarina (PPGD/UFSC, defense expected in 2030) constitutes a clearly bounded strand: Mármore à Margem: Female Allegories and Legal Legitimacy in Brazil (1822–1922), articulated through the theoretical lens of Iconocracia.

Spanning the century between Brazilian Independence (1822) and the Republican Centenary (1922), this strand examines 53 cataloged Brazilian works (coins, banknotes, stamps, civic monuments, forensic architecture, prints, and constitutional frontispieces) alongside their Atlantic counterparts. It investigates the contradiction summarized in the Latin maxim In marmore regnat, in iure tacet («In marble she reigns, in law she is silent»): how the proliferation of sovereign female emblems on pedestals and currency coexisted with the civil incapacity (tutela) and political exclusion of living women.

Core Concepts (Iconocracia)

Visual Sexual Contract · Imago imperat, femina excluditur

Extension of the sexual contract into the field of vision. Female allegory naturalizes in the aesthetic register a pact of exclusion that political theory describes outside the image. Representing woman as an emblem is a way of absenting her as a legal and political subject.

State Femininity · Dea in foro, mulier sub tutela

The State figures itself in the feminine (as Republic, Justice, or Nation) while institutionally organizing the subordination of women: an emblematic femininity that operates as the facade of sovereignty and law.

Visual Racial Contract · Corpus Brasiliae, ius virorum

The constitutive whiteness of the supposedly «universal» allegory: the neutral figure of the State is racially marked, and its neoclassical models circulate through transatlantic transfer. Where the Visual Sexual Contract exposes the gender of the pact, this concept exposes its race: the coloniality of seeing inscribed in the «universal» form.

Classical Purification · Matrix errans, lex immobilis

The formal operation through which the iconocratic regime extracts historical density (biography, materiality, subjectivity) from the feminine figure and fixes it onto an allegorical surface functional to sovereignty. Observable in postural rigidity, the erasure of gesture, and facial standardization, it makes the other three concepts comparable across the archive.

Where the concepts intersect · Veritas temporis filia

The Visual Sexual Contract describes the political economy of the regime: the feminine is summoned as image so that living women remain excluded. State Femininity describes the subjective effect of this economy on the allegorical body. The Visual Racial Contract describes the unequal racial distribution of the same operation. Classical Purification is the formal mechanism that renders all three effects measurable and comparable across the archive. The originality of the research lies in this intersection, not in the novelty of the terms taken one by one.

Method: Constellations, Sources & Experiments

In dialogue with Walter Benjamin and Sigrid Weigel, the atlas treats the constellation as the primary mode of presenting knowledge: figures are read in the interval between manifestations of the allegorical body rather than in isolation or along a linear chronology. Every curatorial montage explicitly states the question that sustains the comparison and preserves the material and historical differences between supports.

At the level of each individual work, the analysis combines three-level iconological reading (pre-iconographic, iconographic, and iconological) with strict archival traceability. Interactive experiments such as Fundição («The Foundry») test the formal indicators of Classical Purification as situated interpretive acts tied directly to the primary archival citations.

Four iconocratic regimes

Works are classified across four regimes, representing analytical repertoires of the allegorical body rather than chronological stages:

  • Foundational: state foundation, independences, revolutions, constitutions. Living, sacrificial, insurgent body.
  • Normative: stabilized bureaucratic supports including stamps, coins, and banknotes (In nummis domina, in legibus ancilla). Domesticated, draped, frontal body.
  • Military: war, imperialism, mobilization. The allegorical body is armed, armored, and monumentalized.
  • Counter-allegory: subversive, satirical, and contested appropriations of the female emblem.

The Open Archive

The corpus is open and expanding: it gathers female allegorical figures with explicit politico-juridical function across coins, stamps, monuments, forensic architecture, prints, frontispieces, banknotes, and posters. Because the supposedly «universal» allegory circulates transnationally, country is treated as an analytical variable rather than an inclusion boundary, allowing the Brazilian 1822–1922 strand to be read against its wider Atlantic and European horizon.

Every item is traceable between its holding archive, its documentary record, and its analytical reading. Reproductions come from the holding institutions; metadata is published under a CC-BY-4.0 license.

Authorship & Research

Ana Vanzin is a doctoral researcher in Law at the Federal University of Santa Catarina (PPGD/UFSC), working on legal history, political iconography, visual culture, and gender. Mnemosyne Viva (iconocracia.com) is the visual and archival atlas of her research. To explore her full curriculum, publications, essays, and personal projects, visit anavanzin.com ↗.
