Repertoire, not chronology

Three dossiers of coexistence

Inscriptio · Muta persona, loquens imago · Discordia concors

This research does not trace a linear arrow from living body to petrified stone. It asks which bodies the State renders available, summons, refuses, or holds in coexistence — for each function, each public, each medium. The three dossiers below show the same allegorical body performing incompatible political work, with the order of dates refuting any evolutionary progression.

I

Columbia, United States

1899 · 1913 · 1917

Columbia hands a sword to an admiral in 1899. Fourteen years later, an actress embodies her in a suffrage pageant to claim the citizenship the figure already represented. Four years after that, she summons army enlistment. The sword comes before the claim — and returns after it.

II

The Republic, France

1871 · 1915

Rops draws the Republic as a satirical, desirable body in the year of the Commune. Steinlen, in 1915, turns the same emblem into a call to arms. Between the two there is no maturation: there is a shift in state function.

III

Brazil

c. 1900 · 1940 · 1961

Villares paints the positivist Republic in a Phrygian cap. By 1940 she is already column and monument at Praça da República in Belém. In 1961 Ceschiatti seats blindfolded Justice before the Supreme Court. Three bodies, three functions of State — none replacing the one before.

Atlas & Doctoral Strand

Mnemosyne Viva & Mármore à Margem

Inscriptio · In marmore regnat, in iure tacet.

«In marble she reigns, in law she is silent. Legal history does not merely obey norms: it inscribes them in images, monuments, and emblems of sovereignty.»

Mnemosyne Viva (hosted at iconocracia.com) is a long-term research project and visual atlas where images, historical sources, concepts, and arguments are explored together. Across every record, the site strictly distinguishes documented objects and archival provenance from authorial interpretations, connecting them through thematic constellations, essays, and interactive experiments.

Within this wider transnational horizon, the doctoral dissertation developed by Ana Vanzin ↗ at PPGD/UFSC — Mármore à Margem: Female Allegories in Brazil (1822–1922), within the theoretical framework of Iconocracia — constitutes a clearly defined strand. From Independence to the Republican Centenary, it investigates why a legal order that excluded living women from civil capacity and suffrage chose the female body to personify the Empire, the Republic, Justice, and the Nation.

How the research reads

Entries, refusals, coexistences

Inscriptio · Patria feminea, civitas masculina

The analysis does not ask which stage of an evolutionary path an image occupies. It asks what operation the image performs: when a female body becomes summonable for a function of State, when the expected body is bypassed or effaced, and when contradictory bodies serve the same State in the same period.

The four regimes — Foundational, Normative, Military, and Counter-allegory — classify each item as an analytical lens, never as stages of a linear progression. Time is evidence of shifting repertoires, not an evolutionary arrow.

Theoretical framework

The concepts and their intersection

Four concepts, and the way they intersect. The originality of the research lies in this intersection, not in the novelty of the terms taken in isolation.

I Concept

Visual Sexual Contract

Imago imperat, femina excluditur

The visual extension of the sexual contract. The female allegorical image naturalizes, in the aesthetic register, a pact of exclusion that political theory describes outside the image: woman is represented as an emblem precisely to displace her historical body.

II Concept

State Femininity

Dea in foro, mulier sub tutela

The State represents itself in the feminine — Republic, Justice, Nation — while institutionally organizing the subordination of women. An emblematic femininity that operates as the facade of legal order.

III Concept

Visual Racial Contract

Corpus Brasiliae, ius virorum

The constitutive whiteness of the supposedly «universal» allegory: the neutral figure of the State is racially marked, and its neoclassical models circulate through transatlantic transfer. Where the Visual Sexual Contract exposes the gender of the pact, this concept exposes its race — the coloniality of seeing inscribed in the «universal» form.

IV Concept

Classical Purification

Matrix errans, lex immobilis

The formal operation through which the iconocratic regime extracts historical femininity — biography, materiality, subjectivity — and fixes it onto an eternal allegorical surface functional to sovereignty. Observable in postural rigidity, the erasure of gesture, and facial standardization, it makes the other three concepts comparable across the archive.

V Intersection

Where the concepts intersect

Veritas temporis filia

The Visual Sexual Contract describes the political economy of the regime: the feminine is summoned as image so that living women remain excluded. State Femininity describes the subjective effect of this economy on the allegorical body. The Visual Racial Contract describes the unequal racial distribution of the same operation. Classical Purification is the formal mechanism that renders all three effects measurable and comparable across the archive.
